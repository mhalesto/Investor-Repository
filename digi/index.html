The Founder's Dilemma
Why This Matters
The Equity Trap
Every VC round permanently transfers ownership. By Series C, many African founders own less than 20% of the company they built. Venture debt offers a way out — for the right company at the right time.
💸
01 · Equity Dilution
Every VC round gives away ownership permanently. After three rounds at 20% each, a founder holds just 51%. Venture debt avoids this — only a small warrant (15–25% of loan value) is issued.
🏦
02 · Banks Don't Work
African banks require 3–5 years profitable trading history, physical collateral, and personal surety. High-growth tech companies rarely qualify for conventional bank debt.
🕳️
03 · The Series B Gap
Only ~10% of African Series A companies raise a Series B. Venture debt bridges the gap — capital to hit metrics needed to raise at a higher valuation.
📌 From the Newtown Partners Venture Debt Playbook (2026): "The most valuable thing you own is your ownership stake. Venture debt lets you fuel growth without permanently transferring equity to investors who may not share your long-term vision."

Why Africa's business models suit debt

Cash-Generative Earlier
African tech operates in real-economy sectors — fintech, logistics, agritech. Companies must be default-alive. Predictable cashflows can service debt from early on.
Linear, Predictable Growth
Infrastructure bottlenecks mean more linear growth curves. Repayment schedules are more reliable — debt structurers can model cashflows with higher confidence.
Trade Sale Exits
84% of African exits are trade sales at 2–5× multiples. More consistent than VC's winner-takes-all model. Structured credit captures this value efficiently.
Speed Advantage
Venture debt closes in 6–10 weeks vs 3–6 months for equity. In fast-moving markets, speed is a genuine competitive advantage.

Anatomy of a Venture Debt Facility

Venture debt is a loan with three distinguishing features: an interest-only period, amortising repayment from cashflows, and a small equity warrant.

The process: first call to funded

Weeks 1–2
Initial screen — submit deck and financials
▼

Rapid quantitative screen: unit economics, ARR trajectory, burn rate, capital structure. Best-in-class lenders (e.g. Newtown Partners' AltSurge platform) return indicative terms within 5 business days. Required: pitch deck, 12–24 months P&L, cap table, use of proceeds plan.

Weeks 2–4
Deep dive — commercial and credit analysis
▼

Full commercial and credit analysis: unit economics, cohort analysis, competitive position, management assessment. Unlike equity DD, debt DD focuses on cashflow predictability, not just growth rate. One structured process — not a rolling data room.

Weeks 4–6
Investment committee — one presentation
▼

You present once to IC. Good lenders include experienced African structured credit practitioners. Approval results in a formal term sheet. Negotiate here — it is much cheaper than negotiating in the full legal documentation phase.

Weeks 6–10
Documentation and close
▼

Legal documentation: facility agreement, warrant instrument, security documents. Conditions precedent must be satisfied before drawdown. Capital typically hits your account within 2–3 business days of CP satisfaction.

Ongoing
Portfolio management — the relationship matters
▼

Monthly financial reporting. Quarterly covenant testing. Strong lenders act as growth partners. Covenant breaches are NOT automatic defaults in a good lender relationship. Communicate proactively and early.

Revenue-Based Finance
A Distinct Instrument
Revenue-Based Finance
RBF is not venture debt. It is a distinct instrument with different mechanics, providers and ideal use cases — and it's growing fast across Africa.

Instead of fixed monthly payments, RBF repayments flex with your revenue. When revenue is high, you pay more. When revenue dips, you pay less. No fixed maturity date — you repay until the total agreed amount is reached.

🔄 The core mechanic: Provider advances $500K. You agree to repay $600K (capital factor 1.2×). Each month, 10% of gross revenue goes to repayment until $600K is repaid. No interest rate. No equity. No fixed schedule.
✅
Ideal for RBF
SaaS / subscription with predictable MRR · e-commerce with consistent GMV · Working capital · Marketing / CAC spend · Companies not yet qualifying for venture debt
❌
Not suited for RBF
Pre-revenue startups · Project-based / lumpy revenue · R&D or team building · Asset-heavy businesses needing secured debt

Hybrid Structures
The Future of African Capital
Hybrid Structures
18% of all 2024 African startup rounds combined debt and equity. Understanding hybrid structures is no longer optional for growth-stage founders.
💡 The hybrid playbook: Raise $2M equity (10% dilution) + $5M venture debt = $7M total. vs. Raise $7M pure equity = ~25% dilution. Same capital raised. Very different ownership outcome at exit.
Equity + Venture Debt
Most common hybrid. Raise a smaller equity tranche, layer debt on top. Reduces dilution while maintaining total capital. Works when company has cashflow to service the debt.
Asset-Backed + Growth Equity
Pledge physical assets (vehicles, solar) for cheap secured debt. Deploy equity only for intangible growth. Moove's model: fleet financed by debt, market expansion by equity.
Receivables Securitisation + VC
Package customer payment obligations into a security. Sun King's $156M Kenyan local-currency securitisation is the benchmark. Unlocks institutional capital at scale.
DFI Concessional + Commercial
A DFI (IFC, FMO, Proparco) provides first-loss capital de-risking a commercial lender's position — enabling larger facilities at better rates. Common in impact fintech and cleantech.
Decision Framework
Decision Tool
Debt vs Equity: The Decision Tree
Answer these five questions in sequence to determine whether venture debt, equity, RBF or hybrid is right for your company right now.
Question 1 · Revenue
Do you have more than $500K ARR?
▼

If NO: Venture debt is not available yet. Focus on equity, grants, or small RBF if MRR ≥$50K. Build to the ARR threshold first — it is the single biggest gate for African lenders.

If YES: You pass the first filter. Proceed to Question 2.

🔢 Why $500K ARR? At this level most lenders can model 18–24 months of cashflows with enough confidence to underwrite. Below this, the error bars are too wide for debt to be sensibly priced.
Question 2 · Predictability
Is ≥75% of your revenue recurring or contractual?
▼

If NO: Lumpy revenues make debt risky. Consider RBF (which flexes with revenue) or equity.

If YES: Strong predictability allows debt to be confidently structured. Proceed to Question 3.

Question 3 · Unit Economics
Is your gross margin above 40% and improving?
▼

If NO: Negative or thin margins mean debt service will consume the business. Fix unit economics before adding debt. Equity is the right instrument — no repayment regardless of margin.

If YES: Growing margins mean cashflows will service debt comfortably. Proceed to Question 4.

Question 4 · FX Risk
Is your revenue in USD, EUR or a stable currency?
▼

If NO (revenue in NGN, EGP or highly volatile local currency): FX mismatch creates serious risk. A 30% naira devaluation effectively increases your USD debt cost by 30%. Structure debt in local currency, hedge, or be very conservative on sizing.

If YES: Clean FX position. Proceed to Question 5.

Question 5 · Purpose
Is this capital for growth, not for survival?
▼

If this capital is to stop the company dying: Do NOT take venture debt. Debt on a struggling company accelerates failure. The covenants will be breached. Seek equity investors who believe in the recovery story.

If this capital is to accelerate a proven model: You have passed all five filters. Venture debt is very likely the right instrument. Proceed to the Debt Readiness Checker.

Wave — Senegal / Francophone Africa

Africa-Specific Risks
Risk Framework
What Can Go Wrong
Venture debt in Africa carries risks that global checklists miss entirely. Understand these before signing any facility agreement.
⚠️ The fundamental challenge: African startups operate in markets where revenue predictability remains elusive — not due to poor business models, but because of infrastructure gaps, regulatory uncertainty, and payment behaviours that swing dramatically on macroeconomic conditions.
FX Mismatch Risk
Borrowing in USD/EUR while earning in naira or shillings creates compounding risk. Nigeria's naira fell 70% vs USD in 2023–24. A $5M USD facility effectively became an $8.5M burden in local terms. Never borrow USD against local currency revenue without a hedging strategy.
Covenant Trap
Covenants that seem conservative at signing become traps during a demand downturn. If minimum ARR is $800K when you're at $1M, one large customer churning breaches the covenant. Negotiate 20%+ headroom and ensure "equity cure" rights and 60+ day cure periods.
Regulatory Change Risk
A single CBN directive can transform a thriving fintech overnight — as multiple Nigerian lenders discovered in 2023. Have a regulatory contingency plan. Ensure covenants account for industry-wide disruptions outside company control.
Collection Delay Risk
Mobile money reconciliation delays and USSD downtime can create 2–4 week gaps between contractual revenue and actual cash receipt — triggering technical covenant breaches even when underlying business is fine. Build a dedicated cash buffer.
Down-Round Interaction
Anti-dilution protection in existing SHAs may interact badly with new shares issued on warrant exercise. A down-round activating anti-dilution ratchets from earlier investors creates unexpected dilution. Review SHA terms before issuing any debt warrants.
Refinancing Risk
A 3-year facility maturing when debt markets are tight forces punitive refinancing (as happened in 2023). Always maintain 2+ lender relationships and begin refinancing conversations 12+ months before maturity.
Exchange Control (SA / Nigeria)
SARB loop regulations and CBN repatriation restrictions can make it difficult to service offshore debt from local operating cashflows. Get a specific legal opinion on the repatriation path before signing any offshore facility.
MAC Clause Risk
Material Adverse Change clauses are deliberately vague and dangerous in Africa given the frequency of macro shocks. Limit MAC to company-specific events only — not macro or industry-wide events — and define materiality thresholds explicitly.

Term Sheet Decoder
Reference Guide
Venture Debt Term Sheet Decoded
Every key term in an African venture debt facility — plain-English definitions with Africa-specific context. Do not sign until you understand all of these.
Interest Rate
Annual cost of the loan as a percentage. Usually base rate (SOFR / JIBAR) plus a margin. Fixed rates provide certainty; floating rates introduce risk.
Africa context: Expect 12–18% all-in vs 8–12% in developed markets. The premium reflects currency, regulatory and liquidity risk. Anything above 18% should be scrutinised closely.
Equity Warrant
A right — not obligation — to purchase shares at the current round price. The equity kicker providing lender upside alongside interest income. Expressed as % of facility size.
Africa context: Standard warrants are 15–25% of facility size. On a $5M facility that is a $750K–$1.25M warrant. Model this in your cap table at different exit scenarios.
Arrangement Fee
An upfront fee paid to the lender for structuring and providing the facility. Typically 1–3% of facility size. Paid once at close — not recurring.
Africa context: A 2% fee on a $5M facility = $100K upfront. Factor into all-in cost of capital. Sometimes negotiable — especially on larger facilities.
Capital Factor (RBF)
In Revenue-Based Finance, total repayment expressed as a multiple of the advance. A 1.2× capital factor on $500K = $600K total repayment.
Africa context: Standard African RBF factors are 1.15×–1.5×. Convert to effective APR for comparison with venture debt — a 1.3× factor repaid over 12 months is approximately 30% APR equivalent.
Tenor
Total duration of the loan facility. Most African venture debt is 3–5 years, including the interest-only period plus amortisation.
Africa context: Push for 4–5 years. 3-year tenors in Africa can be tight given time needed to hit Series B milestones after initial growth targets.
Interest-Only Period
Initial period (typically 12–24 months) paying only interest, not principal. Maximum growth window — cashflow consumed only by interest payments.
Africa context: Negotiate the longest possible IO period. 24 months is ideal; 12 months is the minimum worth accepting. Use aggressively to hit milestones that justify your next equity raise.
Tranche Structure
Splitting the facility into multiple drawdowns conditional on milestones. e.g. $3M at close, $2M when ARR hits $2M. Reduces lender risk and creates natural growth checkpoints.
Africa context: Very common in African deals. Negotiate milestone-linked tranches that align with your business plan — not arbitrary financial metrics.
Security / Floating Charge
Assets pledged as security. Venture debt typically uses a floating charge over all company assets rather than specific asset security.
Africa context: In asset-heavy businesses (fleet, solar), specific asset security enables cheaper debt. Know exactly what you are pledging and how it interacts with future financing.
Financial Covenants
Minimum financial thresholds maintained throughout the loan. Breach triggers lender remedies. Most common: minimum ARR, minimum cash balance, minimum DSCR.
Africa context: Negotiate headroom of at least 20% above base case projections. African businesses face more volatility than Western peers — build this into every negotiation.
Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)
EBITDA divided by total debt service in a period. A DSCR of 1.2× means you generate 20% more cashflow than needed to service debt. Standard minimum covenant.
Africa context: Model DSCR at base, downside (30% revenue miss), and stress (50% miss). If DSCR falls below 1.0× in the stress case, the facility size may be too large.
Cure Period
Time allowed to fix a covenant breach before lender can enforce. Typically 30–60 days. Without a cure period, a technical breach becomes an immediate default.
Africa context: Negotiate at least 60 days and include "equity cure" rights — the ability to inject equity capital to fix a financial covenant breach. Protects against timing breaches (large customer paying late).
Material Adverse Change (MAC)
Allows lender to call the loan if a "material adverse change" occurs in the company's business or prospects. Deliberately vague — which makes it dangerous.
Africa context: Limit MAC to company-specific events — not macro or industry-wide events. Define materiality thresholds explicitly (e.g. >40% revenue decline for >2 consecutive quarters).
Change of Control
Requires lender consent before a controlling shareholder change. Typically triggered by sale of >50% of equity or voting rights.
Africa context: Ensure "control" is defined using SA / Nigerian company law standards. A secondary sale by an existing investor without control should not require lender consent.
Negative Pledge
A promise not to pledge the same assets as security to another creditor without lender consent. Protects the lender's security position. Almost universally required.
Africa context: Ensure equipment finance carve-outs are explicit and generous. Asset-specific financing (vehicles, servers, solar) should be clearly carved out from negative pledge.
Cross-Default
A default under another obligation also constitutes a default under this facility. Multiplies default risk significantly.
Africa context: Limit cross-default to significant financial obligations above a meaningful threshold (e.g. >$500K). A missed $10K equipment lease should not trigger default on a $5M facility.
Prepayment Premium
A fee charged if you repay before scheduled maturity. Compensates the lender for lost interest income. Sometimes called a "make whole" provision.
Africa context: Negotiate a declining prepayment premium (3% Year 1, 2% Year 2, 1% Year 3, nil thereafter). Preserves the flexibility to refinance at better terms if your creditworthiness improves.
